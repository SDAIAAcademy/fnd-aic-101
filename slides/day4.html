Day 3 review

Which split matches use?

A wound-care screen will score next year's visits. The analyst has three years of dated visits, many clients with several. Which split gives an honest test score?

Train on the first two years, test on the third
A random 70/30 split of all visits
Train on the last two years, test on the first
A random split of clients, all years mixed
Why. The screen will meet visits from after its training period, so the test rows must come from after it too.

Answer: train on the past, test on the latest year. The last option is the best of the wrong ones: it fixes the duplicate leak but not the temporal one.

Three families

Each family draws the boundary between flag and pass differently

Linear model

A weighted sum of the inputs; the boundary is a straight line, or a flat plane. Each weight can be read.

Decision tree

Yes-or-no questions on one input at a time; the boundary is a staircase of boxes.

Small neural network

Several weighted sums, each bent, then summed again; the boundary can curve.

All fitted the same way. The line and the network by gradient steps on a loss, as on Day 1; the tree by choosing one split at a time.

Draw the three boundaries on one scatter of walk time against prior falls. More flexibility is not more truth: on tabular screens with a few thousand rows, a linear model is often as good as the others and far easier to check.

Reproducibility

A fixed seed makes a run repeatable, not certain

  • A network starts from random weights; a tree breaks ties at random: the seed fixes those draws
  • Same seed, same data, same library versions: the same numbers, every time
  • Another seed gives a slightly different model: that change is part of the result's uncertainty
  • Two models closer than the seed-to-seed change are tied
What goes wrong. A notebook that only works when its cells are run in one hidden order. Restart and run all before reporting.

The repository pins library versions in a lock file for the same reason. The defence on capstone day re-runs the notebook from a fresh start: this is why.

Checkpoint

What do you report?

On a wound-care screen, a small network scores a validation AUC of 0.873 with seed 1 and 0.866 with seed 2; a logistic regression scores 0.869. Which choice is best supported?

The network, quoting its seed 1 score of 0.873
The network, because its mean is 0.0005 higher
The logistic regression, because they are tied
Whichever scores higher on the test set
Why. The gap between the models is smaller than the network's own change across seeds; when tied, the simpler model is easier to explain and check.

Answer: the logistic regression. The network's mean is 0.8695 against 0.869: no difference worth a word.

Distribution shift

A model meets rows unlike its training rows

New people

A new sector, older clients, a new referral route: the inputs' mix changes.

A changed measurement

A new device, unit or protocol: the same client gets a different number.

A changed outcome rate

More or fewer events than in training: precision and the cheapest threshold move.

The model cannot tell you. It scores whatever it is given. The checks must be built around it.

Ask for one example of each from the room's own work. A changed measurement is the most dangerous because nothing looks wrong to the people collecting it.

Monitoring

Cheap checks catch shifts in the first week

  • Share flagged, by sector and by month, against the training period
  • The mean of each input, by sector and by month, against the training period
  • Recall and precision once the outcomes arrive, months later
  • An owner for each alert, and a person who decides on a pause
A parallel example. A new wound clinic records area in cm², not mm²: every area is 100 times smaller. Its mean area, and its flag rate, would show it on the first day.

The first two checks need no outcomes, so they work at once; recall needs to wait for the outcome window. Ask what a ranking score like AUC would show for the cm² clinic alone: little or nothing. Every area there shrinks by the same factor, so the clinic's visits keep almost the same order among themselves; the flag rate, which compares scores with a fixed threshold, is what moves. Lab 4.2 shows the same: Central's own AUC stayed at 0.724.

Checkpoint

Which check comes first?

A wound-care screen goes live in a new clinic on Sunday. Outcomes take 30 days to arrive. Which check can warn of a problem in the first week?

The clinic's recall at the threshold
The clinic's AUC on its first week of visits
Retraining the model on the clinic's visits
The clinic's share flagged and input means
Why. Flag rates and input means need no outcomes, so they can be compared with the training period from the first day.

Answer: share flagged and input means. Recall and AUC come later, once outcomes arrive.

Proxies

Leaving an attribute out does not remove it

  • A proxy is an input that carries a sensitive attribute: a first name, a home address, a job title
  • Test it: predict the attribute from the input; compare with guessing the larger group
  • An input can also be recorded differently for one group, so the model is weaker for that group even with no proxy
What goes wrong. "The model does not use sex, so it cannot treat men and women differently." Measure, then say.

Give a parallel: a wound screen without age as an input can still separate age groups through a column such as years since registration. Neither effect shows in the list of inputs; both show in the error rates. Do not name the lab's columns before Lab 4.3.

National reference

The SDAIA AI Ethics Principles: fairness through the model's life

  • Preparing input data: sensitive attributes are not model inputs unless there is a justification
  • Building and validating: the chosen model's fairness metrics fall within a defined threshold
  • Deploying and monitoring: the predefined fairness metrics are monitored in production
Course rule. Also test for proxies of a sensitive attribute. The English edition names this step and the Arabic edition does not, so the course states it as its own rule.

The model card

One page that says what the model is for, and for whom it is weak

  • Intended use and out of scope
  • Data, split and metrics, against a baseline
  • Metrics by group, with counts
  • Known limits and who should not rely on it
  • Monitoring: what, how often, by whom

Prepared, reviewed, accepted

The analyst prepares it; a different person reviews it; the owner of the decision accepts the model for use.

Adapted from Mitchell and colleagues' Model Cards for Model Reporting (2019). The template is labs/model_card_template.md and the capstone uses it. The separation of roles is required in every reference answer: the person who builds a model does not approve it.

Checkpoint

What does the model card say?

A wound-care screen has recall 0.82 for clients aged 75 to 84 (17 long visits) and 0.72 for 65 to 74 (25 long visits). What belongs in its model card?

The overall recall only, since the groups are small
Both rates with their counts, and a note that the gap is uncertain
A statement that the screen is biased against younger clients
A plan to add age as an input to fix the gap
Why. The card reports what was measured and how much it can bear; the counts show that these rates move a lot by chance.

Answer: both rates with counts and a note on uncertainty. Reporting is not the same as concluding; the reviewer and the owner decide what to do.

Where language models fit

A language model learns like a supervised model, on text, at a vast scale

  • Features: the text so far. Label: the next piece of text, taken from the text itself
  • Fitted by the same idea as Day 1: gradient steps that make a loss small
  • The family is a very large neural network; the data are huge amounts of text

The same limits

Leakage (test questions seen in training), shift (new topics), and gaps between groups apply here too.

Next. FND-AIC-102 How Language Models Work takes this further.

Keep this to five minutes. The point is continuity: nothing about a language model escapes held-out testing, baselines or leakage. DS-204 is the next step for tabular models: boosted trees, tuning and calibration.

The capstone

A modelling report on a new screen: unplanned admission in 90 days

  • Split by date, a baseline and two models in pipelines
  • A leak check, and a threshold from the stated costs and base rate
  • A one-page model card with an owner and a different reviewer
  • A public repository, its history kept; a ten-minute defence

AI and data

AI-full for the capstone's code; you explain every line. Into any generative AI tool, only public or synthetic data.

The brief is labs/capstone/README.md. Only Public data goes into generative AI tools, never anything classified Restricted or higher, as SDAIA's guidelines for government require; here the data are synthetic. The defence re-runs the notebook and explains a result the instructor picks.

How the capstone is scored

Five criteria, 100 points

CriterionPoints
Held-out workflow, end to end with a fixed seed25
Leakage-safe data handling15
Threshold decision from costs and base rate20
Model card with subgroups, limits, owner and reviewer20
Defence: re-runs and explains a selected result20

Each criterion scores a different thing, so nothing is counted twice: the threshold is scored once, the model card once. The capstone is 40% of the course mark.
