Model complexity

A flexible model can learn the noise as well as the pattern

  • A tree's depth is how many questions it may ask; each extra level doubles the possible groups
  • With enough depth, every training row gets its own leaf: training error reaches zero
  • Overfitting: training score keeps rising while the validation score falls
  • Underfitting: both scores are low; the model is too simple to show the pattern

In the case

Two clients with the same walk time and the same falls can have different outcomes. A tree that separates them is memorising chance.

Ask: "If a tree gets every training row right, what has it learned?" Draw a scatter with a smooth boundary and a wiggly one. The wiggly one fits every point and will be wrong on the next client.

Regularisation

A penalty on large weights keeps a model simpler

  • Add to the loss a cost for large weights: the model trades fit for simplicity
  • In scikit-learn's logistic regression, C is the inverse: small C, strong penalty
  • For trees, the penalty is a limit: max_depth, min_samples_leaf
  • The strength is chosen on validation, like depth

Same picture, other dial

Strong penalty: underfit. No penalty with many features: overfit. The best setting sits between.

The lab fits a logistic regression on many polynomial features to make overfitting visible, then turns C. Warn people that C runs the opposite way from what they expect. Regularisation is why a network of millions of weights can still generalise; Day 4 returns to that.

Checkpoint

Learning curves

Training and validation scores as the rows grow

Good fit

The two curves meet at a useful score. More rows help a little.

Overfit

Training stays near perfect, validation well below. More rows or a simpler model help.

Underfit

Both curves meet low. More rows do not help; better features or a richer model do.

A question to ask. Will collecting more assessments help? The curve answers before anyone collects them.

Sketch each shape on the board. The lab gives six curves to classify. Watch for people who call any gap overfitting: a small gap that is closing is a good fit.

Checkpoint

Is the new model better?

On the wound-care screen, five grouped folds give a mean AUC of 0.927 with a spread of 0.019. A new model scores 0.931 on the same folds. What do you conclude?

The new model is better and should replace the old one
The new model is worse, because it is more complex
Regroup the folds by sector and score again
They are tied; prefer the simpler one
Why. A difference of 0.004 is well inside the fold-to-fold spread of 0.019, so it may be chance.

Answer: tied, choose the simpler. This rule comes back in Lab 4.1, where a small network ties with a logistic regression.

The confusion matrix

Four cells: caught, needless, missed and correctly passed

FlaggedNot flagged
Fellcaught (true positive, TP)missed (false negative, FN)
Did not fallneedless (false positive, FP)correct pass (true negative, TN)
A threshold makes the flag. A model gives a score from 0 to 1; flag when the score is at or above the threshold. Change it and the four cells change.

Use the screen's own words: caught, needless, missed, correct pass. "Positive" means flagged, not good news. Ask which cell the physiotherapy team lead worries about (needless: capacity) and which the clinical director worries about (missed).

Rates from the cells

Precision and recall answer two different questions

Recall = TP / (TP + FN)

Of those who fell, the share we flagged. Low recall: fallers missed.

Precision = TP / (TP + FP)

Of those we flagged, the share who fell. Low precision: programmes wasted.

  • Specificity = TN / (TN + FP): of those who did not fall, the share left alone
  • Accuracy = (TP + TN) / all: dominated by the larger group when one outcome is rare

Say each formula as a sentence. A screen for a rare outcome can have high accuracy and miss almost everyone; the next slide shows it on wound care.

What does this screen miss?

The default threshold of 0.5 is not a decision anyone made: it is a library default. With a base rate of 0.16, few scores reach 0.5. The question is now: what threshold?

Error costs

Choose the threshold with the lowest expected cost

  • Give each error a cost in the same units: a missed outcome C_FN, a needless flag C_FP
  • Expected cost = C_FN × FN + C_FP × FP, per 1,000 clients
  • Try thresholds on validation; keep the cheapest; report it once on test
  • The owner of the decision sets the costs; the analyst shows the trade-off
A rule of thumb. If scores are well calibrated, flag when the score exceeds C_FP / (C_FP + C_FN).

In the case, the clinical director estimates a missed fall costs about six times a needless programme: a figure invented for the case, not a clinical or economic estimate (the capstone uses eight to one and the follow-up five to one, set the same way). The analyst does not pick the costs. The rule follows from comparing the expected cost of flagging, (1 − p) × C_FP, with not flagging, p × C_FN.

Rule and search can differ. The search on validation found 0.1; the rule of thumb, 1 / (1 + 4), says 0.2. The rule assumes calibrated scores.

Costs 4 for a missed long visit and 1 for a needless slot.

The cheapest threshold flags more visits. The physiotherapy team lead would ask whether the team can handle 16% flagged: capacity is a real constraint the cost model may leave out. The lab asks for the fall screen's own threshold.

Base rates

The same screen gives a different precision where the outcome is more common

  • The base rate is the share with the outcome, before any model
  • Same scores, more fallers: more of the flagged are fallers, so precision rises
  • The cheapest threshold moves too, because misses happen more often

What goes wrong

A precision measured in one sector is quoted for another with twice the base rate. Report the base rate with every precision.

This prepares Day 4's later batch, where a new sector arrives. Ask: "A screen has precision 0.30 where 10% fall. What happens where 30% fall?" It rises, with the same model.

Day 2 · wrap-up

Three things to keep

Choose on validation

Training scores always favour flexibility. The validation peak is the choice.

Scores have a spread

Grouped folds show how much a score moves; smaller differences are ties.

Costs set the threshold

Four cells, two costs and the base rate decide what to flag; 0.5 is a default, not a decision.

Ask each table for one sentence they would say to the clinical director about the threshold. The debrief of Lab 2.4 opens Day 3.
