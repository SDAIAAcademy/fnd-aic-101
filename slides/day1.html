Each row is an example whose answer we already know: the last column. Ask: what would you need to know before the visit to guess the last column? That is the whole idea of supervised learning. The fall-risk data of the labs has the same shape; we keep its numbers for the labs themselves.

Three kinds of column

Features go in, the label comes out, the identifier stays out

Features

What is known when the prediction is made: wound area, layers, diabetic. Written X.

Label

What you want to predict, known for past rows only: ran over 40 minutes. Written y.

Identifier

Which visit or client a row is about. Needed to join and audit, useless as a predictor.

Ask of every column. Is it known at the moment the prediction is made? If not, it is not a feature, however well it predicts.

The callout is the seed of Day 3's leakage session; plant it now. Example: "minutes the visit actually took" predicts "ran over 40" perfectly and is useless, because it is known only after the visit.

A prediction rule

A rule is a function from features to a prediction

def rule(visit) -> bool:
    return visit["area_cm2"] >= 15
  • On the six visits it flags 4 and catches all 3 that ran over
  • One flag was needless: the 28.5 cm² wound with one layer
  • A model is the same thing, written by a fitting procedure from many rows instead of by you from six

Write the rule on the board as "if area at least 15, flag". Then say: machine learning does not change what a rule is; it changes who writes it and from how many examples. Counts from teaching_examples.py, section D1 S1.

Scoring a rule

A screen makes two different mistakes

A miss

The visit ran over and the rule did not flag it. For a fall screen: a client who falls and got no programme.

A needless flag

The rule flagged a visit that was fine. For a fall screen: a programme for a client who would not have fallen.

What goes wrong. Counting only "right answers" mixes the two, and a rule that flags nobody can look excellent when the outcome is rare.

Ask the room which mistake is worse for the fall screen. Let two people disagree; on Day 2 you will put a cost on each and the disagreement becomes a number.

From writing rules to fitting them

A loss turns "how wrong is this rule" into one number

  • Pick a family of rules with a few numbers in it: a line minutes = w × area + b
  • For one setting of w and b, compute the error on every example
  • Average the squared errors: the mean squared error
  • Fitting means finding the numbers with the lowest loss
Same idea, every model. A line, a logistic regression, a neural network and a language model are all fitted by making a loss small.

The family is chosen by a person; the numbers are chosen by the procedure. Squaring keeps positive and negative errors from cancelling and makes large errors count more.

The shape of the loss

For a line, the loss is a bowl over w and b

The tangent at the red point slopes down to the right, so the step goes right.

Draw this on the board too. The slope of the loss at the current point says which way is downhill. That slope is the derivative from the pre-work: for the squared error it is 2 times the mean of error times x for w, and 2 times the mean error for b.

Gradient descent

Step against the slope, by a small rate

e = w * x + b - y               # errors
slope_w = 2 * mean(e * x)
slope_b = 2 * mean(e)
w = w - rate * slope_w
b = b - rate * slope_b

From w 1.0, b 15 on the five visits

slope_w = -56.0, slope_b = -4.0. With rate 0.002 the step gives w 1.112, b 15.008 and a loss of 1.93, down from 6.00.

The exact bottom

Least squares gives w 1.125, b 15.5 and a loss of 1.50. More steps keep closing the gap.

Walk through the signs: the slope in w is negative, so subtracting it increases w. Numbers from teaching_examples.py, D1 S2. In the lab, participants do two such steps by hand on the fall data's ten rows, then two thousand in code.

The one setting

The learning rate decides whether the steps arrive

Too small

Every step is safe and the walk takes forever.

About right

The loss falls fast, then flattens as the slopes flatten near the bottom.

Too large

Each step jumps over the bottom to a higher wall; the loss grows without end.

What goes wrong. A loss curve that rises or turns into "nan" almost always means the rate is too large or a feature has huge values.

The stretch of Lab 1.2 makes the loss explode on purpose. The link to scaling (Day 3): a feature with large values makes its slope large, so it sets how large a rate can be.

Reading a loss curve

Plot the loss after every step and read three things

  • Falling: the steps are working
  • Flat: you are near the bottom, or the rate is far too small
  • Rising or jagged: the rate is too large
  • Use a log scale on the loss axis: the early drop is huge, the late gains small

This is the first of the curves participants learn to read; Day 2 adds complexity and learning curves. In the lab the loss starts near 1,757 for the line that predicts zero minutes; ask them to predict before they run it how far it will fall.

Two ways to use examples

Keep the examples, or keep a summary of them

Nearest neighbours

Keep every row. For a new case, find the most similar rows and let them vote. No fitting; all the work happens when you predict.

A linear model

Fit a few numbers once, then throw the rows away. Prediction is a sum of features times weights.

Compute one distance aloud: (9, 4) to (10, 1) is the square root of 1 + 9, about 3.16. The number of neighbours, k, is a choice: k = 1 trusts a single row, a large k drifts towards the majority.

The trap in distances

Change a unit and the neighbours change

  • Record the same wounds in square metres: 10 cm² becomes 0.001 m²
  • Area differences shrink to almost nothing; layers decide every distance
  • The three nearest become the rows with one or two layers: rows 1, 6 and 3 in place of 5, 2 and 1
  • Nothing about the wounds changed, only a unit
What goes wrong. A distance adds raw differences, so the column with the biggest numbers wins. Scale the columns first (Day 3).

The vote happens to stay at "will not run over" here, but the neighbours are different rows, which is enough to distrust the method without scaling. In the lab, recording the walk in tenths of a second changes the vote for both new clients. Rows are numbered from 1 as in the table.

A line by hand

For one feature, the best line comes from means

w = Σ (x − mean x)(y − mean y) ÷ Σ (x − mean x)²

b = mean y − w × mean x

The bottom of the bowl, found in one move instead of many steps.

The five wound visits

mean area 12, mean minutes 29. The formula gives w 1.125 and b 15.5: the same line gradient descent was walking towards.

Show the columns you would write on paper: x minus its mean, y minus its mean, their product, the square of the first. The formula exists only for a straight line with a squared error, which is why gradient descent is the general tool. Numbers from teaching_examples.py.

Generalisation

What matters is the score on clients the model has not seen

  • A model is built from past rows and used on future clients
  • Its score on its own training rows is always flattering
  • Hold some rows back, never fit on them, and score there
  • The gap between the two scores is the first thing to look at (Day 2)

Bring back Lab 1.1: the rule looked better on the ten rows it came from than on 1,000 others. The held-out set formalises that experience.

Three sets, three jobs

Train on one, choose on another, report on a third

Training

Every model is fitted here. Seen as often as you like.

Validation

Compare models and settings here. Each choice makes its score a little flattering.

Test

Opened once, after every choice. Its score is the one you report.

What goes wrong. Look at the test score, change something, look again: the test set has become a second validation set.

Ask why two held-out sets are needed. If validation is used to pick the best of five models, the winner's validation score is the best of five draws, so it is optimistic. The test set took no part in any choice.

Split by date when the model will meet the future

The wound-care visits: 24 months, split at month 14 and month 19 into 1,390, 535 and 475 visits.

A random split mixes months, so the model is tested on months it has partly learnt from. For a screen that will be used next year, the honest question is how a model built on the past does on what follows. Day 3 shows what a random split hides. The fall data uses the same idea: training to mid-2024, validation to the end of 2024, test the first half of 2025.

The baseline that predicts the majority class scores 0.914 because 92% of visits do not run over 50 minutes. The model adds 2.6 points of accuracy. Is that a lot? Accuracy cannot say; the next slide gives a score that can.

A score for ranking

AUC: how often a positive case outscores a negative one

  • A model gives each row a score between 0 and 1
  • Pick one visit that ran over and one that did not, at random
  • AUC is the chance the first gets the higher score
  • 0.5 is a coin; the do-nothing baseline scores exactly 0.5
  • The wound-care logistic regression: 0.968

AUC stands for area under the ROC curve; you do not need the curve today, only the meaning. It ignores the cut-off, which is why it can show that a model separates cases even when its accuracy at 0.5 is close to the baseline's. Choosing the cut-off is Day 2. Number from teaching_examples.py.
